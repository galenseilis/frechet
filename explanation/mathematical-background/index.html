    Mathematical background¶

    This page explains why the package works the way it does. Read it if you want to understand the mathematics, the notation, and the design decisions — not if you just need to call a function (the how-to guides are better for that).

    The problem: reasoning from marginals alone¶

    Suppose you know the probability of each event in a family A_1, …, A_n but you do not know how the events relate to one another. The events might overlap heavily, be nearly disjoint, or anything in between. The Fréchet inequalities give the tightest bounds on the probability of their intersection and union that hold for every possible joint distribution consistent with those marginals.

    This is what makes the package useful for zero-data analytics: when a summary statistic is available but the raw data is not, you can still make a defensible statement rather than guessing.

    The two inequalities¶

    There are two such inequalities. One bounds the intersection of events, and the other bounds the union.

    \[ \begin{align} \max \left( 0, \sum_{k=1}^n \Pr[A_k] - (n-1) \right) \;\le\; & \Pr \left[ \bigcap_{k=1}^n A_k \right] \;\le\; \min_k \left\{ \Pr[A_k] \right\}, \\ \max \left\{ \Pr[A_k] \right\} \;\le\; & \Pr \left[ \bigcup_{k=1}^n A_k \right] \;\le\; \min \left( 1, \sum_{k=1}^n \Pr[A_k] \right). \end{align} \]

    The lower intersection bound is the inclusion–exclusion principle pushed to its limit: if the marginals sum to more than 1, the excess must lie in the overlap. The upper union bound is the complementary fact: the union can never exceed one, nor the sum of the parts.

    A note on notation¶

    You may notice that these docs use the set-theoretic symbols ∩ and ∪ rather than the logical connectives ∧ and ∨. In many settings they can be used interchangeably, but formal probability theory is defined on sets, so we prefer the set-theoretic notation.

    The independence gap¶

    What it measures

    The independence gap quantifies statistical dependence. The Fréchet inequalities put lower and upper bounds on how much dependence there could be, given only the marginal probabilities.

    The independence gap was conceived of sometime between asking A formal definition of a "measure of association" and posting this answer to that question. It was originally defined in terms of cumulative distribution functions, but Sextus Empiricus readily generalised it to events from any probability space in their answer to Is the pairwise independence gap bounded to \([-1/4, 1/4]\)? What about for n variables?.

    For events E_1, …, E_n from a suitable probability space (Ω, 𝓕, P), the independence gap is

    \[ \phi[E_1, \ldots, E_n] \triangleq \Pr\left[ \bigcap_{k=1}^n E_k \right] - \prod_{k=1}^n \Pr[E_k]. \]

    Statistical independence of the collection is exactly the equality

    \[ \Pr\left[ \bigcap_{k=1}^n E_k \right] = \prod_{k=1}^n \Pr[E_k], \]

    so the gap measures independence directly from its definition:

    • a value of zero means the events are statistically independent,
    • a positive value means the joint probability is larger than independence would predict,
    • a negative value means the joint probability is smaller.

    Because the Fréchet bounds on the intersection always contain the product ∏ Pr[E_k], the resulting bounds on the gap always cover zero. That is why you cannot use it to rule out independence.

    Ruling out independence is impossible

    One might naïvely hope that the bounds could rule out statistical independence, but they cannot. The bounds will always cover zero.

    The almost-disjoint gap¶

    What it measures

    The almost-disjoint gap quantifies how close a collection of events is to being disjoint (mutually exclusive). The Fréchet inequalities provide bounds that can be normalised into a score.

    In Can we obtain a probability interpretation on these bounds obtained from the ADG and Frechet inequalities?, the almost-disjoint gap (ADG) is defined as

    Quote

    The almost-disjoint gap is given by

    \[ \mathcal{D}_{\mu} \left( E_1, \ldots, E_n \right) \triangleq \mu \left( \bigcup_{k=1}^{n} E_k \right) - \sum_{k=1}^n \mu \left( E_k \right), \]

    where μ is a measure and {E_k} are elements of a suitable σ-algebra. When the ADG equals zero, the events are almost everywhere pairwise disjoint.

    The inclusion–exclusion principle states that

    \[ P\left( \bigcup_{k=1}^n E_k \right) = \underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^n P(E_k)}_{\text{marginal terms}} + \underbrace{\sum_{k=2}^n \left((-1)^{k-1} \sum_{\substack{I \subseteq \{1, \ldots, n \} \\ |I| = k}} P\left(\bigcap_{i \in I} E_i \right) \right)}_{\text{joint terms}}. \]

    This shows directly what the ADG means: it is exactly the sum of the joint terms.

    \[ \mathcal{D}_{P} \left( E_1, \ldots, E_n \right) = \underbrace{\sum_{k=2}^n \left((-1)^{k-1} \sum_{\substack{I \subseteq \{1, \ldots, n \} \\ |I| = k}} P\left(\bigcap_{i \in I} E_i \right) \right)}_{\text{joint terms}}. \]

    Combining the ADG with the Fréchet union inequality bounds the ADG in terms of the marginals:

    \[ \max \{ P(E_k) \} - \sum_{k=1}^n P ( E_k ) \;\le\; \mathcal{D}_{P} \left( E_1, \ldots, E_n \right) \;\le\; \min \left(1, \sum_{k=1}^n P(E_k) \right) - \sum_{k=1}^n P ( E_k ). \]

    Not knowing the marginals, the most extreme bounds depend only on the number of events:

    \[ 1 - n \;\le\; \mathcal{D}_{P} \left( E_1, \ldots, E_n \right) \;\le\; 0. \]

    Normalising the almost-disjoint gap¶

    Since 1 - n ≤ 0, we can flip the bounds by multiplying by -1:

    \[ 0 \;\le\; -\mathcal{D}_{P} \left( E_1, \ldots, E_n \right) \;\le\; n-1. \]

    Because n - 1 can exceed one, divide through by n - 1 to normalise onto the unit interval:

    \[ 0 \;\le\; \frac{-\mathcal{D}_{P} \left( E_1, \ldots, E_n \right)}{n-1} \;\le\; 1. \]

    Where this is useful¶

    The primary applications are in data analysis where the appropriate summary statistics are available but the raw data is not. Typical situations include auditing reported figures, sanity-checking a model against partial information, or reasoning about a system you cannot access directly.

    Design philosophy¶

    Fréchet is designed to work with minimal dependencies. Because the primary use case is to work with summary statistics, dealing with truly "big data" is out of scope. The package therefore uses no special packages or language extensions — only the Python standard library.

    Is Fréchet compatible with PyPy?

    Fréchet appears to work with PyPy, but this is not a supported compatibility guarantee.
